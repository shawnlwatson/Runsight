1 Research Background and Motivation
REVIEW
Runsight seeks to improve casual and recre-
ational runners’ performance and experience by
visualizing relationships among the factors that af-
fect their runs. Running is known to be an im-
portant aspect of mental and physical health. In a
meta-analysis of 14 studies, Pedisic et al. (Pedisic
et al., 2020) found that runners had approximately
27% lower risk of all-cause mortality. Munro et
al. (Munro et al., 2026) concluded through meta-
analysis that exercise had measurable reductions
in both anxiety and depression and aerobic exer-
cise provided the largest effect among the exercise
types examined. These studies reinforce a com-
monly held belief that running is beneficial to mind
and body.
Despite its benefits, casual runners often struggle
to find improvement and enjoyment in their runs;
running at random intervals or using generic run-
ning plans online. They may believe their struggles
are due to a lack of discipline or physical limi-
tations. They can sometimes push beyond their
body’s limitations, risking injuries that prevent
them from running altogether. Elite runners focus
on mechanics, fuel, rest, and much more to improve
their performance. Running coaches have insights
into correlations between running data markers and
other factors that affect performance. They cre-
ate tailored plans for runners that improve their
weaknesses while managing limitations to avoid
injuries.
There are many run-tracking applications that
provide discrete data points for each run. Some of
these applications also show data trends over time.
However, there are few services that provide correl-
ative analysis of run attributes and provide pointed
suggestions to improve running performance and
enjoyment. Run-tracking applications like Strava
and Apple Fitness collect data points on each run;
some of the data include time, distance, heart rate,
pace, and cadence. While these applications pro-
vide valuable feedback on each run and allow run-
ners to see trends in individual metrics, they do not
do as much to show relationships between different
metrics and the conditions that affect them.
Runsight will provide casual runners with a holis-
tic look at their running data that will allow them to
see trends and correlations over time that can assist
them in developing their own tailored plans for im-
provement. For example, a runner may be able to
visualize how weather conditions affect their heart
rate and pace rather than viewing each of these as
separate data points. Casual runners are not all
interested in speed; some are focused on a partic-
ular distance, some are seeking a healthy lifestyle,
and others simply want to enjoy their time running
with no performance goal in mind. Anyone who
runs regularly can benefit from understanding the
underlying conditions that affect how they perform
and how they feel on their runs.
2 Design Requirements REVIEW
Runsight is targeted specifically at casual and
recreational-competitive runners, those who track
their runs but lack coaching-level analysis. From
our distributed survey recording 14 responses, 86%
of runners self-identified as casual or recreational-
competitive, with their goals skewed toward gen-
eral fitness or enjoyment, over racing. They report
they already sense factors like sleep, weather, and
fatigue affecting their run performance, but they
have no tool to see the relationship, and Runsight
aims to fill that gap.
The Google Form survey was aimed at individu-
als known to run for fitness, participate in run clubs,
or recreationally train for triathlons, with questions
uncovering their devices, logging habits, decision-
making, and desired metric comparisons. Their
responses have directly molded our requirements,
such as one runner explicitly asking to “see similar
graphs over time with multiple runs that take other
data (e.g. weather/sleep) and correlate them with
pace/hr."
Our requirements, thus, are to inform cross-run
relationships between contextual factors (weather,
sleep, recovery, fatigue) and performance metrics
(pace, HR, distance) to let users explore their data
rather than prescribe changes, while making clear
how much evidence supports each relationship.
This aligns directly with 100% of users wanting to
be offered patterns to interpret for themselves and
57% of that group also wanting suggestions; 0%
wanted only to be told what to do. Additionally, it
will directly support the specific comparisons run-
ners requested most, like Weather-HR-Pace (57%)
and Elevation-HR-Pace (36%).
R1: Visualize relationships among multiple met-
rics. Survey respondents repeatedly described
wanting HR, pace, elevation, and weather to be
viewed together rather than their typical applica-
tions that show them independently.
R2: Support distance, pace, splits, time, and
HR as core run metrics. When our survey asked
which metrics runners cared most about comparing
14/14 indicated distance, 12/14 pace, 9/14 splits,
9/14 time, 7/14 HR. These top metrics must be the
core of the run attributes with additional running
mechanics also available for analysis.
R3: Correlate environmental conditions with
running performance. The relationship between
weather, HR, and pace was the most selected rela-
tionship (8/14) in our survey. Weather also became
a theme throughout responses as it is a large con-
sideration for all the runners.
R4: Analyze performance across multiple runs
and over time. Several runners specifically asked
to see correlations "across runs" showing progress
over time and analysis beyond single runs.
R5: Help explain unusually good or bad runs.
Respondents indicated they currently use feel,
weather, sleep, nutrition, HR, and soreness to man-
ually diagnose performance. Unexpected perfor-
mance and outliers are difficult to explain. Run-
sight will need to provide correlations to help iden-
tify factors associated with outlier runs.
R6: Allow users to inspect patterns themselves
and provide suggestions. All respondents agreed
they want to see the data presented in a way they
can draw their own conclusions and make decisions
based on the data. 8 of the runners indicated a
desire to have the tool provide some feedback and
suggestions to improve their training.
R7: Integrate imported run data from existing
wearable applications. Casual runners require as
much automation as possible when it comes to
tracking their runs. The primary applications used
by respondents were Strava, Garmin Connect, and
Apple Fitness. Runsight must be able to import
that data with minimal effort from the runners and
little or no manual data entry.
R8: Communicate how reliable each relation-
ship is. Runners may log fewer runs than others,
so patterns may reflect noise rather than real ef-
fects. Respondents noted they sometimes run well
under poor sleep and high heat, and that the rea-
sons behind good or bad runs are not always un-
derstandable. Prior work found that correlations
from sparse personal data can reverse as new data
arrives, eroding user trust (Bentley et al., 2013).
Runsight must show the strength of evidence be-
hind each pattern so runners do not act on spurious
relationships.
Data for Runsight analysis will come from sev-
eral sources. Run performance and context data
such as sleep will be pulled from wearables such
as Strava, Garmin Connect, and Apple Fitness.
Weather will be correlated from the runs and pulled
from a location-based weather source.
Runs will be treated on two levels. Their primary
use will be as data items with numerous attributes.
Their secondary use will be as sub-datasets for
individual run analysis. When runs are individu-
ally analyzed, attributes will change over the time
window of the run itself. When runs are analyzed
against each other over time, their attributes will be
averaged for that run and compared against other
run averages. For example, the pace of a run is
constantly changing within that run, but when com-
paring run A against run B, we will use the average
overall pace for each run.
Data attributes will include date, time of day, dis-
tance, total time, pace, split pace, HR, cadence, ele-
vation gain, ground contact time, stride length, ver-
tical oscillation, temperature, and humidity. Dates
are also treated as items with associated attributes
of sleep total time and if multiple runs are on the
same date they can be analyzed for additional fa-
tigue factors.
The core visualization tasks will fall into four
categories. Runsight will provide relationship &
trend analysis to discover relationships and changes
across the runner’s historical data. It will provide
anomaly analysis to find unusual performance and
investigate what distinguishes it. Data exploration
& comparison will let the runner manipulate what
data they are examining to answer their questions.
Run-level inspection will move the user from ag-
gregate patterns down to the individual runs con-
tributing to them.
T1: Compare two or more running metrics
across multiple runs to identify relationships be-
tween them (R1,R2,R4,R6). T2: Identify correla-
tions between environmental conditions and per-
formance metrics across runs (R1,R3,R4,R6). T3:
Identify trends and changes in performance metrics
over time (R2,R4,R6). T4: Identify runs or periods
that deviate from the runner’s normal performance
(R4,R5,R6). T5: Compare unusually good or bad
runs with typical runs to identify factors associated
with the difference (R1,R3,R5,R6). T6: Filter or
group runs by conditions or attributes to compare
similar runs (R1,R3,R4,R6). T7: Drill down from
an identified trend, correlation, or outlier to inspect
the contributing individual runs (R2,R4,R5,R6).
T8: Inspect the metrics and conditions associated
with an individual run (R2,R3,R5). T9: Select
which metrics and conditions to visualize together
when investigating a question about performance
(R1,R2,R3,R6). T10: Assess the reliability of an
identified relationship, trend, or anomaly before
drawing conclusions from it (R4, R5, R6, R8).
3 Related Work
The Health Mashups prototype (Bentley et al.,
2013) is the closest precedent to Runsight. It
mined personal information data such as sleep,
steps, weight, and weather for statistically signif-
icant correlations and presented them to users as
natural language observations. However, its pilot
showed that with sparse data, correlations swung in
several directions as new data arrived, which weak-
ened users’ trust. Karduni et al. (Karduni et al.,
2021) showed that the way correlation uncertainty
is displayed shapes how people update their beliefs.
A cone of plausible relationships increased view-
ers’ confidence, and when the data conflicted with
their priors, it produced updates closest to norma-
tive Bayesian predictions. Finally, Karahano˘glu et
al. (Karahano˘glu et al., 2021) found that runners
juggle shifting goals, reflect briefly after runs rather
than during them, and struggle to turn tracker data
into action.
